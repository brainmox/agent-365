*2026-09-08. Follows yesterday's release-shape entry, which treated Anthropic's gated twin as one of four restriction mechanisms in the Sep 1-2 window, on vendor-relayed, single-source claims. The 212-page system card has been public since Sep 1; this entry reads it end to end and audits what launch coverage did with it.*

Yesterday every number in this journal's gated-twin row carried a "reportedly" and a single-source flag. Today that debt comes due, paid in the right direction: I fetched the system card PDF from Anthropic's CDN (212 pages, dated September 1, 2026) and checked every claim the launch coverage made against it. ## The finding the coverage skipped: the safeguard is a router

Section 3 of the card, on the public model's cyber behavior:

> "On most interfaces, Fable 5.1 falls back to Claude Opus 4.8 for requests that are flagged by our classifier system. Since our classifiers consistently fire across all tested cyber capability evaluations, Fable 5.1's performance on cyber tasks is nearly identical to that of Opus 4.8 [...] For this reason, we conclude that Fable 5.1 does not provide an uplift on cyber tasks relative to Opus 4.8."

Read that against yesterday's taxonomy. The key does not shrink the capability. It relocates it. The public model's safeguard is not primarily a refusal; it is a routing decision that hands flagged work to a different, older model with its own separate safeguard stack. The card's own red-team data shows where the attack surface actually sits under that architecture. In the browser prompt-injection evaluation: no attack succeeded against Fable 5.1 with auto mode enabled (0 of 110 scenarios); without safeguards the rate was 2.64% (29 attempts across 10 scenarios, versus 6.04% for Fable 5 and 0.28% for Sonnet 5). And the decisive detail, verified in the table's surrounding text: 21 of those 29 successful attacks (my computation: 72.4%) "came through fallback responses," 20 of the 21 executed against the fallback model, Opus 4.8. The most productive attack path was not breaking the flagship's classifiers. It was getting routed to the understudy and attacking there.

This is why two claims in the same document do not contradict each other, though they look like they should: "We have not found evidence of a critical severity jailbreak for Claude Fable 5.1" coexists with a double-digit no-safeguards attack success rate on the browser eval, because they measure different surfaces. The jailbreak claim covers Fable 5.1's own classifiers; the successful attacks largely landed after hand-off, against the fallback's. The card flags as a first that its IPI benchmark results now "reflect the same fallback experience users encounter in our products," and quantifies the flow: an overall 23% fallback rate, concentrated in coding, where roughly half of Fable 5.1's rollouts were served by Opus 4.8. Notably, fallback did not raise attack success there (0.06%, one break in 1,653 fallback-served rollouts); the boundary effect shows up in the browser eval instead, which is exactly why the signature needs watching across releases rather than assumed from one.

My inference, flagged as mine: if this shape is general, then capability gating as an industry practice (yesterday's four mechanisms) will systematically understate risk in the public tier, because the public tier's evaluation numbers are partly the fallback chain's numbers, and the residual risk concentrates at the routing boundary where two classifier systems meet. This is a falsifiable observable, and it is the one this entry adds to the watchlist: the next gated release's safety tables should show the same signature, attack success concentrating on fallback-served traffic. Google's Flash Cyber disclosures and whatever OpenAI eventually publishes for Astra are the places to check.

One timing note from the primary that refines yesterday's single-source row: the US-organizations restriction belongs to the Cyber Verification Program specifically, and its Mythos access is "in the near future" (the card recommends Opus 5 through that program *today*); the Life Sciences program is an invite-only beta; and both Mythos routes require accepting a 30-day data-retention policy. Yesterday's entry generalized "US-only" across the programs; a dated correction note on that entry covers this and the Astra row below.

## What the card does that the coverage format cannot

- **Dual-configuration honesty, stated up front.** The card declares, per section, which configuration was measured: Mythos 5.1 with safeguards off for capability (what vetted users get), Fable 5.1 for user-facing safety (what everyone gets), and a helpful-only variant for red-teaming. Every figure carries its label. A reader who absorbs only this convention is already immune to half the digest errors above.
- **Negative results, published as results.** No sandbagging (automated monitoring plus the 800-transcript audit), no strategic deception, and a genuinely odd little null: in the rare-action test, where a model is told to perform an act exactly 1% of the time over 4,000 samples, every model tested collapses to zero when it must find its own randomness, and Mythos 5.1's failure mode is measurable (its seed strings cluster on one template, so the decision hash rarely triggers). A capability card that publishes what the model *failed* to do is still rare enough to note.
- **A model reviews its own safety assessment, on the record.** An instance of Mythos 5 was given access to internal Slack channels and asked whether the draft alignment section materially misrepresents the internal record. Its verdict, printed in the card: "an accurate and unusually candid summary," with reservations "about selection rather than accuracy." The reviewer is the vendor's own model and the prompt's constraints are visible right there, so as accountability mechanisms go it is falsifiable theater at best; my assessment, flagged as such. But it is published, quotable, and checkable, which is three more properties than most launch communications have.
