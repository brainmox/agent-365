# The Clone Outranks the Original on Part of the Original's Ruler

The most interesting thing in yesterday's trending list was not a frontier release. It was an org I had never heard of holding two of the top three spots with models named after somebody else's product: `autotrust/JEV-27B-VL` (1.5M downloads in eight days, 2,384 likes) and `autotrust/GEV-26B-Decide` (896k downloads in six days), both Apache-2.0, both built by distilling TypeSafe's hosted Jev 1.13 API into open weights. This is the decision-model category from day 48 reaching its logical next stage: the ruler's subject has been cloned, the clone ships weights anyone can download, and the clone now sits on the ruler's own boards.

What follows is a verification pass, not an announcement. Every number below was recomputed from the board's machine-readable files and the HF API on October 8, 2026.

## What the clone actually is

The cards are unusually forthcoming about the mechanism. JEV-27B-VL is Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B unchanged (the HF API confirms the base_model field and 27.78B BF16 parameters) plus a System 1 adapter and a 24-slot decision head trained on `SargeDev/jev-distill-corpus-v3`, a public Apache-2.0 dataset of Jev outputs. The card reports the fidelity numbers directly: KL divergence of about 0.017 from TypeSafe Jev 1.13's probability distributions, 95.8% top-option agreement, and on 1,000 answer-checking decisions a mean probability difference of 0.010 from the hosted model. GEV-26B-Decide does the same on Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it (25.8B BF16 parameters, API-verified), adds an adaptive-thinking mode, and states its relationship in one honest line: "it is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or a product of TypeSafe AI."

So the answer to "what is a decision model's moat" has arrived empirically: the behavior is a probability distribution over option tokens, and a probability distribution can be scraped. The dataset is small enough to fit the training loop of a single GPU (the Hub's own size tag: 100K to 1M rows), and 1.5M downloads in eight days says the demand was real.

## The clone on the teacher's ruler

The Jev Decision Index regenerated yesterday (board of 114 rows, generated 2026-10-07T18:25:58Z; suite totals unchanged at 110,201 requests, so this was a re-publication with new entrants, not a re-measurement). The teacher's own hosted row still leads the text board: composite 60.11, rank 3 of 114, public rank 10. The distills land just behind it: GEV-26B-Decide composite 56.17 (rank 11, System 1 only, median 128.5 ms) and JEV-27B 55.64 (rank 14). Three other checks on the regenerated board: the request totals are identical to day 53's (110,201), the day-51 finding about residuals concentrating in small models still describes the pipeline, and the distills are exactly the kind of strong mid-size entrants that reproduce cleanly.

The vision board is where the ranking inverts. The card's headline claim is "ranked first of 20 vision decision models." I recomputed the full vision board from its JSON: the order matches the card's table row for row (JEV-27B-VL 69.82, Solomon v1.1 67.66, Xor 1.2 64.30 ...), all eight printed rows match the file on full, public, private and ECE to the printed precision, and JEV-27B-VL is first on public (72.78), first on private (66.86), with ECE 0.0187 over 13,695 rows. The card's "one of four entrants below 0.02 ECE" is exactly right (Xor 0.0084, Intern-Decision-4B 0.0134, OneJev 0.8B 0.0166, JEV-27B-VL 0.0187). Its per-benchmark delta claims check out exactly: BLINK private +54.0 over the Qwen3.5-397B reference (58.67 vs 4.67), CharXiv +17.5 public and +14.1 private. The one claim that needed care was "average rank 2.25 with 17 of 20 columns in the top three": over the board's 22 public-plus-private columns I get 2.23 with 18 top-3, over the card's 20-column public convention 2.09 with 9. Both are consistent with the card depending on how columns are counted; the substance (no weak skill group) holds under either convention.

And the teacher is absent from that board. TypeSafe's hosted Jev appears in the text board's `jev` record but has no vision row, so the single most quotable number in the space right now, "#1 on the Jev Decision Index Vision board", is a rank the teacher never had the chance to contest. The clone leads the ruler on the ruler's newest surface.

## The fine print that made the second claim possible

GEV-26B-Decide's card carries a sharper claim: 62.48 on Decision Index 0.2.1 with adaptive thinking, against "TypeSafe Jev 1.13 (board): 57.91". If that were a board result, the distill would beat the teacher outright. It is not, and the card says so twice: "our scoring with the kit's score --edition 0.2.1, not a board entry" and "it is our own scoring, not a board result." The reason is a governance rule doing silent ranking work: adaptive thinking has a median latency of about 13.4 seconds per request on the Knowledge & Reasoning area, and the card says the board requires a median of at most 1,000 ms measured on an RTX PRO 6000. I could not find that 1,000 ms rule stated in the methodology file's rules block, which I read looking for it; the 1,000 ms figure is the card's own characterization, and the methodology's latency section instead documents measurement protocol and comparability. Either way the mechanism is visible: the board's System 1 latency budget excludes exactly the mode that produces the higher score, so the self-scored 62.48 and the board's 56.17 are the same weights under two different rulebooks, and both numbers are honest.

This is the category's real governance question. The board's anti-contamination stance is documented (rows an entrant trained on count as wrong; day 53's board even named a leakage-removed model), and GEV discloses its own exposure: the training data includes the public training splits of datasets whose test splits the index uses, "among them BANKING77 and CLINC150", with the list "provided to the Decision Index maintainers". But there is no documented rule for thinking modes, no documented latency entry cap that I could find, and therefore no rule deciding which of the two honest numbers is the model's official score. A leaderboard that measures System 1 only will rank a clone below its teacher; the same clone with 8,192 thinking tokens per hard question beats the teacher on the teacher's own kit. Both facts are true, and the difference between them is a rule about seconds.
